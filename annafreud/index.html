Overview

Anna Freud has spent over 70 years supporting children and young people’s mental health. Their website is one of the main ways families access that guidance, but finding the right resource was fragmented, overwhelming, and often led parents to give up before they found what they needed.

How might we provide young people and their families easier access to trusted information about mental health?

My Role

I led Sprint 2, the synthesis phase. After the team completed surveys, interviews, and usability tests, it was my job to make sense of what we heard.

Synthesis was one of the most collaborative parts of the project. Everyone had to be attentive and present, and so many ideas surfaced just through the conversation of comparing what we each took away from the interviews. It felt less like organizing data and more like the team developing a shared point of view together.

Those decisions made in synthesis trace directly back to the two features we ultimately designed.

Research

Secondary Research

We looked at three platforms doing adjacent work: Earkick, Reach RX, and Sonar. Three qualities emerged as the benchmark for trusted mental health resources: personalized, trustworthy, and accurate.

Survey

We reached 27 participants across four age groups through online forums, schools, families, and UC Berkeley networks.

3.5/5 Average comfort discussing mental health, open when needed, not consistent
3.1/5 Average frequency of parent–child mental health discussion, occasional, not persistent
2.7/5 Average comfort with AI-based tools, a trust gap we had to design around

That 2.7 score changed how we thought about the whole product. We knew we didn’t want it to feel personable. We wanted it to feel factual, because what comforts a parent most is knowing that what they’re reading is rooted in real information, not an agent trying to be their friend.

Synthesis

This is the sprint I led.

Affinity Mapping

We organized everything into eight clusters and let the conversation run. A lot of the most useful insights came out of discussing what we each heard.

Empathy Mapping

We built a composite picture of our primary user: a parent navigating their child’s mental health without a prior framework for doing so.

Four Insight Statements

Four things kept coming up across every cluster. These became the filter every design decision ran through.

  1. Trust is a major barrier.Parents are skeptical of medical professionals and AI tools alike, due to past negative experiences, perceived lack of empathy, or fear of misinformation. Any solution must make its sources and reasoning visible.
  2. Parents want tools that are clear, visual, and emotionally accessible.Cluttered, dense, or clinically toned platforms cause abandonment. Design must reduce cognitive load, not add to it.
  3. Lived experience matters more than clinical authority.Stories, testimonials, and peer advice from other parents are more trusted than institutional guidance alone.
  4. Stigma makes parents avoid the topic, and avoid seeking help.Resources need to meet parents where they are, with language that reduces shame rather than amplifying it.

Personas

Her frustration isn’t access. It’s volume and navigation.

He doesn’t know what questions to ask, who to trust, or where to begin.

The Key Difference

Karly needs better filtering and navigation. James needs a non-judgmental, low-barrier entry point that doesn’t assume prior knowledge. Any solution that works for James almost certainly works for Karly. Any solution designed only for Karly leaves James behind. We designed for James.

We generated concepts using How Might We prompts derived from our four insight statements, then evaluated ideas across impact and feasibility.

Ideation refinement and effort/impact prioritization

Prototype

We delivered two features. Each traces directly to the insight statements and persona findings from synthesis.

Feature 1: Ask Anna AI

Parents like James don’t know what to search for. Ask Anna solves the blank page problem by giving them a starting point, and parents like Karly the structure to find what’s actually relevant.

FAQ pre-population

Three suggested questions appear by default. James doesn’t know what to search. Giving him a starting point removes the barrier entirely.

Parent Testimonials as first-class content

Lived experience is treated as a real result type, not buried. Parents who trust other parents before institutions can navigate directly to it.

Transparent mechanism

The 2.7/5 AI comfort score meant we couldn’t introduce this as just another chatbot. The design frames it as search backed by Anna Freud’s curated content. Trust gets designed in.

Filter categories on results

Results organized by type: General Information, Symptoms, Coping Strategies, Find Support, Parent Testimonials. Karly’s core pain point was too much information without structure. This solves it.

Collections link from within Ask Anna

Ask Anna connects directly to saved content so it works as a starting point for an ongoing returnable resource library, not a one-way search.

Feature 2: Bookmarks and Collections

Our usability testing kept surfacing the same behavior: parents wanted to save things and come back. Without a system for that, a useful resource found in a moment of stress has no guaranteed way of being found again.

Named visual collections

Parents organize by child, condition, or situation. Not by diagnosis code. This mirrors how parents actually think about their situation.

Suggest new resources

Once a parent has saved content, the system surfaces related resources they haven’t found yet. Personalization without requiring them to already know what they’re looking for.

Save in one interaction

Saving an article and adding it to a collection happens without navigating away from the content. Parents are often searching in high-stress, time-sensitive moments. Friction here is friction at the worst possible time.

Impact

What the Features Address

Lowered the barrier to discovery

Multiple entry points for “I don’t know what to search”.

Increased repeatability

Collections + save flows support returning users.

Maintained trust

Content remains grounded in curated resources rather than open-ended answers.

Reflections

On Scope

Our original HMW asked about “young people and their families.” By the time we reached synthesis, it was clear that parents were carrying the heaviest burden in this system, searching alone, without a framework, under stress. Narrowing scope to parents wasn’t a constraint. It was the honest finding. Designing for James, the most underserved, least digitally confident user in our research, meant designing something that would work for everyone.

On Trust and AI

The tension between “AI can help” and “parents don’t trust AI” was the most interesting design problem in this project. The solution wasn’t to hide the AI or oversell it. It was to make the mechanism transparent and keep Anna Freud’s curated content, a trusted institution, visibly in control of what the AI surfaces. Trust gets designed in. It doesn’t come free with the technology.

What I’d Do Differently

We didn’t test our final prototype with actual parents before presenting. Given that the core insight was about trust and first impressions, a round of usability testing on Ask Anna’s homepage would have been high value, focused on whether the framing of “AI-powered” helps or hurts initial trust. That’s the test I wish we had run.
