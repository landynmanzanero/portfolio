Overview

Baro is a web platform helping K-12 teachers implement evidence-based behavior interventions for individual students. I led a six-person engineering team through authentication, role-based dashboards, and the early intervention onboarding flow.

This is not a design case study. But it’s where I learned that design decisions don’t wait for designers.

Milestone 1 Authentication

Teacher accounts with email/password and Google OAuth. Student login via teacher-generated codes. Data collection layer for onboarding.

Milestone 2 Role-Based Dashboards

Two entirely separate dashboard experiences. Teachers see their students. Students land on a personalized welcome screen before entering their own view.

Milestone 3 (Partial) Intervention Onboarding

Reduced screen count, scroll-based flow, save progress. Data modeling for the behavior categorization system that determines reinforcement schedules.

The Brief

The client came in with two artifacts: a behavioral science spec document and a set of Figma files. The problem was they didn’t consistently agree. Screens were redundant, flows required unnecessary clicks, and the onboarding sequence was broken into far more steps than the logic actually required.

Before we could build, someone had to decide which version of the product we were building. That role fell to me.

What I Built

Milestone 1, Authentication

I built teacher account creation and login with email and password plus Google OAuth, secure session handling, and the student login mechanism using a unique code generated on the teacher side. I also built the data collection layer for teacher onboarding, capturing grade levels, teaching position, years of experience, and three self-rating scales that would later feed into intervention recommendations.

Milestone 2, Role-Based Dashboards

Two entirely separate dashboard experiences: teachers land on a Prep view showing their student cards, students land on a personalized welcome screen. The routing logic required clarity on one design question first: what is the most important thing each user needs to see in the first three seconds? For teachers, it’s their students. For students, it’s acknowledgment that this space is theirs.

Milestone 3, Intervention Onboarding

The client’s Figma showed a long click-next sequence, one question per screen, with a “Next” button to advance. We restructured it as a scroll-based flow and added a save progress button. The reasoning was simple: teachers are interrupted constantly. An onboarding flow that loses progress when a teacher gets pulled away is a flow that never gets completed. The save button wasn’t in the spec. It came from thinking about the actual context of use.

What Leading Looked Like

Six engineers building together without clear design direction produces six different products. My role was to prevent that.

In practice that meant facilitating working sessions where the team could surface design questions before they became code problems. That process slowed down individual work slightly and made the final product significantly more coherent. It also meant the team started asking better questions of the client.

Learnings

Authentication and data modeling are design decisions.

The structure you choose for your database determines what the product can do later. Technical choices have design implications, whether or not a designer is in the room.

Specs and Figma files are starting points, not answers.

The client’s deliverables described what they wanted the product to do. They didn’t describe what it would feel like to use it. That gap is where design lives.

Technical leadership is a communication problem.

The engineering decisions I’m most proud of on this project came out of conversations, not solo problem-solving. Getting six people to build one coherent thing requires a shared understanding of what that thing is for. That’s a design skill applied to a team.
